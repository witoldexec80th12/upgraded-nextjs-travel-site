## A granddaughter of empires

She was, by any measure, an unlikely mistress for a grey Transylvanian fortress. Marie was born in 1875 an English princess — a granddaughter of Queen Victoria on her father's side and of Tsar Alexander II of Russia on her mother's — raised amid the nurseries of Europe's grandest houses and married, at seventeen, to Ferdinand, the shy heir to the young Romanian throne. She might have been merely decorative. Instead she became the most vivid personality Romania had known in generations: beautiful, headstrong, theatrical, a painter and a writer, a woman who wore folk costume and Byzantine gold with equal conviction and who understood, long before her ministers did, the power of a queen who could be *seen* to love her country. Romania, in turn, adored her — and the story of how she came to possess Bran is really the story of how a borrowed English girl made herself utterly Romanian.

## The nurse and the Great Reunification

The First World War was the crucible. When Romania entered on the Allied side in 1916 and was almost immediately overrun, the royal family fled to Iași. Marie refused the comfort of exile. She worked the military hospitals herself — the photographs show her in nurse's uniform, kneeling on ward floors — and in doing so became the human face of a catastrophe that might otherwise have broken the country's will entirely. When peace came and the diplomats gathered at Versailles and Trianon, she travelled to Paris to argue Romania's case in person, charming Allied leaders who had never expected to negotiate with a queen. The result was Greater Romania: Transylvania, Bessarabia, Bukovina, and Dobruja all united in a single state.

It was in that spirit of gratitude that the city of Brașov, on Romania's first Unification Day — 1 December 1920 — presented her with the keys to the old fortress at Bran. She accepted them with characteristic drama: "It was given to me as a surprise and I simply fell in love with it on the spot."

## Transforming a fortress into a home

The castle she inherited was a medieval watchtower: functional, austere, full of cold stone and tiny windows. What she made of it, with the architect Karel Liman over the following decade, is one of the more remarkable acts of personal reinvention in the history of European interiors. Arrow-slits became proper windows. A wooden covered staircase in the courtyard was enclosed in a round tower. A secret staircase was cut directly into the rock, connecting her bedroom to the courtyard below. She added a well-house, a tea house, a small wooden church, a house for her daughter Ileana, and — this being the nineteen-twenties — electricity, central heating, a telephone exchange, and a hydraulic elevator built into the hillside with a tunnel running beneath the courtyard.

The furnishings came from everywhere: Byzantine icons she had collected since childhood, oak furniture from her English grandmother's houses, embroidered folk textiles bought in village markets, painted wooden objects from Transylvanian craftsmen. The effect, still visible today, is of a place shaped by a single powerful aesthetic sensibility rather than assembled by committee — warm, slightly eccentric, unmistakably hers.

## A scent of violets

She loved the place so completely that she wished a part of herself to remain in it forever. When Marie died in 1938, her heart was removed at her own request and set, in time, in a small chapel at Bran — though the heart, like the queen, was fated to wander: carried across shifting borders through the turbulent decades that followed, it rests today in the room where she died, in the palace of Pelișor, and the little Bran chapel keeps only the memory of it. Yet those who walk the castle's upper corridors still tell of a curious thing. Now and again, in empty rooms where no flowers stand, there drifts the sudden, unmistakable scent of **violets** — Marie's favourite flower, her signature perfume. The staff smile and say it is only the Queen, come back to see how her house is keeping.

Believe it or not as you please. But when you climb Bran today and find, amid all the fortress stone, a room full of light and warmth and a window where an arrow-slit should be, you will know exactly whose hand was here — and why, of all the castles a queen might have chosen, she gave her heart to this one.
