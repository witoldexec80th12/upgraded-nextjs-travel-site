After room upon room of carved walnut and coloured glass, Peleș changes its tone. Turn a corner and the soft glow of the salons gives way to the cold gleam of steel — helmets, breastplates, halberds, whole walls bristling with edges. This is the armoury, and for a great many guests it is the moment a visit tips from admiration into delight. Children who have been dutifully quiet through the porcelain wake up here. So, if we are honest, do their parents.

## A collection on a serious scale

Peleș's visionary, Carol I, did not assemble a decorative handful of swords for effect. He built one of the finest arms collections in this part of Europe — around four thousand pieces in all, spanning roughly the fourteenth to the nineteenth centuries. A royal inventory taken in 1948 counted nearly four thousand nine hundred weapons and medals together, which gives some sense of the true depth once everything in store is counted.

Not all of it stands on show at once. The **Grand Armoury** alone holds some sixteen hundred European pieces, in halls fitted out between 1903 and 1906; a separate, smaller armoury is given over to the arms of the East. It is a great deal to take in, and — as with the rest of Peleș — no one will manage it in a single pass.

## The knight who came on horseback

Every armoury has its star, and Peleș's stands in full: a complete tournament collection of the early sixteenth century, armour for not just the knight but for his horse as well, the pair fitted out as if to ride tomorrow.

It is a *Maximilian*-type armour, named for the Holy Roman Emperor — recognisable by the crisp parallel fluting that ripples across the steel. Those flutes were no mere ornament; they stiffened the plate and helped turn a blow aside, the way corrugation strengthens a sheet of metal. The knight's own harness runs to some twenty-five or thirty kilograms, the left side reinforced against an opponent's lance, a rest bolted to the breastplate to bear its weight.

If you look closely, the image of the sixteenth-century knight in action sharpens in the imagination: the metal carries old dents and deformations — the marks of genuine impact, of a harness that was worn and struck and not merely admired. This is no costume. Someone, five centuries ago, rode in this.

## A treasure carried down from a father's castle

Here the collection folds back into the story of the man who built this castle. That great horse-and-rider harness is understood to have come from the collection at Sigmaringen — the Hohenzollern seat in south-western Germany where Carol was born, in 1839, and raised — carried south to the Carpathians from the very walls of his childhood. The homesick prince who dressed his mountain castle in the German manner also brought a piece of his father's armoury from his birthplace to hang upon its walls — an act of care and a further expression of Carol's thumbprint on this landscape.

Nor was the king careless about what he owned. His arms were catalogued in a manuscript inventory drawn up by one Julius Scheürer — the kind of scholarly bookkeeping that marks a true collector rather than a mere gatherer of trophies.

## Steel from East and West

The finest European work here is generally reckoned to be the German plate of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. But the reach of the collection is the point. Alongside the crossbows and polearms sits a grim fifteenth-century German executioner's sword; in the smaller armoury, the curved and jewelled weapons of the Ottoman, Persian, and Arab worlds — and even a Japanese blade — speak to a king who collected across every border he could reach.

It is the same instinct that furnished the salons with rugs from Bukhara and porcelain from Saxony: a nineteenth-century monarch gathering the whole world beneath one very well-appointed roof.

## The room guests don't expect to love

There is a particular pleasure, for us, in watching visitors meet the armoury. Many arrive braced for a genteel tour of a royal house and find instead a hall full of knights and warhorses and the honest menace of old steel. It is the room that turns a polite outing into a memorable one. And though we are always working to improve a guest's visit, we are not surprised to hear the same gentle complaint again and again — that there was never quite enough time inside. There is simply too much to marvel at.

Take your time with the horse and rider. Stand where you can see the dents in the plate. In a castle this beautiful it is easy to treat the armoury as a transition passage between finer rooms — but for many guests, this is the corner of Peleș they remember longest.
