There is a sentence we hear so often at Peleș that we have quietly adopted it as our own: beautiful outside, stunning inside. Guests who believe the façade has already impressed them step through the doors and find they were only beginning. If the mountain-and-forest silhouette is the overture, the rooms are the opera — and they were never designed to be quiet.

What defines our interior is its density. Peleș is not so much decorated as encrusted: nearly every surface has been carved, inlaid, painted, gilded, or hung with something. There is more here than a single visit can hold, and it is worth choosing your entry time carefully so your eyes have room for all the detours they will want to take.

## The kingdom of wood

If one material governs these rooms, it is wood. Walnut above all, carved until it seems less built than grown — panelling, staircases, ceilings, whole rooms sculpted from it. 

Queen Elisabeth, the poet-queen who wrote under the name Carmen Sylva, took a guiding hand in the interiors and asked for exactly this: wood, richly and endlessly sculpted, warm, yet richly Carpathian, where an alternative like marble would have been cold.

The Viennese decorator Bernhard Ludwig dressed the grandest rooms, and the finest workshops of Europe supplied the rest — furniture from Nuremberg, silver from Paris, bronzes from Berlin and Munich. To stand among it is to be surrounded, quietly, by the craftsmanship of an entire continent.

## A journey through nations, without leaving our walls

Stepping through the rooms of Peleș transports you across history and geography. Each principal space takes on the manner of a different country or age. A single afternoon becomes a spellbinding cultural tour.

At the heart of the house is the **Hall of Honour**, a full-height atrium rising some sixteen metres, panelled in walnut and exotic timbers and worked with bas-reliefs. Its finest device waits overhead: a great stained-glass ceiling that slides open at the touch of an electric motor, letting the mountain sky fall into the room — a piece of theatre as provocative as it was technologically advanced.

From there the nations unfold. The **Moorish Salon**, designed by the French architect Charles Lecomte du Noüy, cools the eye with a marble fountain modelled on one in Cairo. The **Florentine Room** answers in Italian Renaissance, lit by candelabra of Murano glass. The **Turkish Parlour** wraps the visitor in Ottoman textiles; the **Imperial Suite** puts on Austrian Baroque, in tribute to Empress Maria Theresa.

Two rooms reward a slower pace. The Queen's **Music Room**, panelled in teak, holds stained glass that tells Romanian fairy tales in coloured light — a private, storybook corner within a very public house. And our small **theatre**, all Louis-XVI elegance and barely sixty seats, keeps a genuine surprise overhead: its ceiling and friezes were painted by a young Gustav Klimt, working with Franz Matsch, years before the world learned his name. We hope you will look up. It is easily passed by, and far too good to miss.

## The things inside the rooms

Even once the rooms themselves have been taken in, the objects within them begin their own quiet work. The stained glass was fired chiefly in the celebrated Zettler workshops of Munich — the labour of some forty artists over three years — with further panels from Vienna.

Underfoot lie carpets from Bukhara, Mosul, Smyrna and beyond; on the shelves, Sèvres and Meissen porcelain; on some walls, tooled leather from Córdoba; and throughout, a collection of paintings numbering close to two thousand, gathered by our first king with the eye and the appetite of a serious collector.

None of it has the air of a museum's careful hoard. Carol I drew from India, China, and Japan as readily as from Paris and Vienna, and what pleases us still is how little any of it jars — the far-flung pieces sit as though they had always belonged. This was, after all, a home; it simply happened to have the resources of a king behind its furnishing.

## Left as it was

Part of what visitors feel here is owed to the castle's condition. Peleș came through the twentieth century — expropriation, the communist decades, long years of uncertain custody — remarkably whole and undamaged. Guests remark, almost in disbelief, that they can find neither a scratch nor a speck of dust; the rooms keep the air of having been left only yesterday, the family merely stepped out. It lends a visit a strange intimacy, as though one were trespassing, very politely, on a life still being lived. Capacity limits and changes to ticketing come from a real concern: if we wish to leave Peleș immaculate for the next generation, we must continue Carol I's attention to detail and steward it forward.

## A word before you come

If we may offer a little counsel, as those who know these rooms best: you will not take it all in on one pass. The house is simply too full, and any route through it too brief, for that. The guests who come to love Peleș most are the ones who slow down — who linger beneath the Klimt ceiling, who find the fairy tales in the Music Room's glass, who let the sheer accumulation settle over them rather than trying to catalogue it.

Many arrive on a day-trip from Bucharest, their minds still half on vampires in some other set of hills, expecting to spend an efficient ninety minutes with us. More often than not they leave quietly astonished, already wondering how to come back for longer. Beautiful outside, stunning inside — our guests said it first, and we are content to let them keep saying it.
